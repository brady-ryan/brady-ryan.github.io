Lensed quasars and the Expansion of the Universe

A galaxy in the foreground splits a distant quasar into several images. Light from each image traverses a different path as it travels towards Earth, so a change in the quasar reaches us at different times. Measure those delays and you can weigh the universe's expansion rate!

From time delays to the Hubble constant

Each delay is the product of two components. The first is a geometric factor governed by the gravitational potential of the lens. The second, known as the time-delay distance, is controlled by distances between the lens, quasar, and Earth, and scales as 1/H0. Measure the delay, model the lens, and H0 follows directly, without the cosmic distance ladder or the cosmic microwave background.

Shaded bands: Planck 2018 cosmic microwave background (67.4 ± 0.5) and SH0ES 2022 Cepheids plus supernovae (73.0 ± 1.0), in km/s/Mpc. The dashed line is the true value in this simulation.

Why quasars

A quasar is an extremely distant active galactic nucleus, whose brightness oscillates on timescales of days to months. That makes it ideal for timing, as each image is sharp enough to measure separately.

Reading the overlays

The gold critical curve marks infinite magnification; images merge or split as they cross it. In the magnification map, blue areas keep the source’s orientation and orange areas mirror it.
